## 1. The Billiard System

A **dynamical billiard** is a point particle with mass $m$ (set to 1) and speed $v$
(set to 1) moving freely inside a compact planar domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$,
reflecting off the boundary $\partial\Omega$ according to the specular reflection law.

The Hamiltonian in the interior is purely kinetic:

$$H(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) = \frac{|\mathbf{p}|^2}{2}, \qquad \mathbf{q} \in \Omega.$$

At the boundary a hard-wall constraint is imposed: $q \in \partial\Omega \Rightarrow$
the normal component of momentum is reversed (see §2).  The energy $H = \tfrac{1}{2}$
is therefore conserved, and the speed $|\mathbf{v}| = 1$ is constant throughout.

### Why billiards are non-trivial

Between consecutive collisions the trajectory is a straight line — there is no force
and no potential energy.  All complexity comes from the geometry of $\partial\Omega$.
Changing the shape from a circle to a stadium replaces a completely integrable system
with a completely chaotic one.

## 2. The Billiard Map

### Specular reflection

Let $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ be the outward unit normal to $\partial\Omega$ at the collision
point $\mathbf{q}$.  The incoming velocity $\mathbf{v}_\text{in}$ maps to

$$\boxed{\mathbf{v}_\text{out} = \mathbf{v}_\text{in} - 2\,(\mathbf{v}_\text{in}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}.}$$

This preserves the speed ($|\mathbf{v}_\text{out}| = |\mathbf{v}_\text{in}| = 1$) and
the tangential component ($\mathbf{v}_\text{out}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}} =
\mathbf{v}_\text{in}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}$), and only reverses the normal component.

### Discrete-time map

Because the motion between collisions is trivial (straight line at unit speed), the
entire dynamics is captured by the **billiard map**

$$T: \Sigma \longrightarrow \Sigma, \qquad
T(\mathbf{q}_n, \mathbf{v}_n) = (\mathbf{q}_{n+1}, \mathbf{v}_{n+1}),$$

where $\Sigma = \{ (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{v}) : \mathbf{q} \in \partial\Omega,\;
|\mathbf{v}| = 1,\; \mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} \geq 0 \}$ is the
**collision cross-section** (positions on the boundary paired with outgoing velocity
directions).  $\Sigma$ has dimension 2.

### Generating function

$T$ is a symplectomorphism generated by the **length functional**: if $\mathbf{q}_n$
and $\mathbf{q}_{n+1}$ are two consecutive collision points, then

$$p_n = -\frac{\partial\,|\mathbf{q}_{n+1} - \mathbf{q}_n|}{\partial s_n}, \qquad
p_{n+1} = \frac{\partial\,|\mathbf{q}_{n+1} - \mathbf{q}_n|}{\partial s_{n+1}},$$

where $s$ denotes arc-length along $\partial\Omega$.  This is Fermat's principle of
shortest path in disguise: the billiard trajectory minimises total chord length
subject to the boundary constraint, and the canonical momentum conjugate to arc-length
is $p = \sin\alpha$ (the sine of the incidence angle).

## 3. Birkhoff Canonical Coordinates

Every collision is parameterised by two numbers:

| Symbol | Name | Range | Definition |
|--------|------|-------|------------|
| $s$ | arc-length | $[0, L)$, $L = |\partial\Omega|$ | distance along $\partial\Omega$ from a reference point |
| $p = \sin\alpha$ | momentum | $(-1, 1)$ | tangential velocity component at collision |

Here $\alpha \in (-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2)$ is the **angle of incidence**, measured
from the inward normal, so

$$p = \mathbf{v}_\text{out}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}, \qquad
\hat{\mathbf{t}} = R_{\pi/2}\,\hat{\mathbf{n}} \quad
(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \text{ rotated } 90° \text{ CCW}).$$

$p > 0$: CCW motion relative to the boundary; $p < 0$: CW; $p = 0$: perpendicular
incidence; $|p|\to 1$: grazing incidence.

### Area preservation (Liouville)

The billiard map is **area-preserving** in $(s, p)$ coordinates:

$$T^*(\mathrm{d}s \wedge \mathrm{d}p) = \mathrm{d}s \wedge \mathrm{d}p, \qquad
|\det DT| = 1 \text{ everywhere.}$$

This follows from Liouville's theorem for the underlying Hamiltonian flow.  A direct
consequence: the invariant measure on $\Sigma$ is $\mu = \mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}p /
(2L)$, and the measure of any region of phase space is preserved under iteration of
$T$.  In particular, **billiards have no attractors**.

## 4. Integrable Billiards: The Circle

### Conservation of angular momentum

For a circular billiard of radius $R$ the angular momentum

$$L = R\,|\mathbf{v}|\sin\alpha = R\,p$$

is conserved at every reflection.  This follows from the rotational symmetry of the
domain: by Noether's theorem, symmetry $\Rightarrow$ conserved charge.

### Exact billiard map

Because $p$ is invariant, the only non-trivial part of the map is the update of $s$.
An elementary geometric argument (isoceles triangle $O\mathbf{q}_n\mathbf{q}_{n+1}$
with $|O\mathbf{q}_n| = |O\mathbf{q}_{n+1}| = R$) gives the central angle
$\angle(\mathbf{q}_n O \mathbf{q}_{n+1}) = 2\alpha = 2\arcsin(p/R)$, so

$$\boxed{s_{n+1} = s_n + 2R\arcsin\!\left(\frac{p}{R}\right) \pmod{2\pi R},
\qquad p_{n+1} = p_n.}$$

This is a **rigid rotation** of the circle by angle $2\arcsin(p/R)$.  The dynamics
is entirely determined by the **rotation number**

$$\varrho = \frac{\arcsin(p/R)}{\pi} \in (0, 1),$$

and the orbit type by whether $\varrho$ is rational or irrational:

| $\varrho$ | Orbit type | Trajectory |
|-----------|------------|------------|
| $\varrho = k/m$ (rational) | **Periodic**, period $m$ | Closes after $m$ bounces; forms a regular $m$-gon |
| $\varrho \notin \mathbb{Q}$ | **Quasiperiodic** | Never closes; collision points are dense on $\partial\Omega$ |

### Caustics

Every chord of the trajectory is tangent to the **caustic circle** of radius

$$r_c = R\cos\alpha = R\sqrt{1 - p^2}.$$

The caustic is an invariant curve in configuration space: once $p$ is fixed, every
subsequent chord touches $r_c$.  In the Poincaré section $(s, p)$ the caustic
corresponds to the horizontal line $p = \text{const}$, the signature of an
integrable system.

### Poincaré section of the circle

The section is a **foliation of horizontal lines** $\{p = c\}$ for $c \in (-1,1)$.
Each line is an invariant 1-torus (a circle in 1D phase space).  The circle billiard
has the maximum possible number of invariant tori — it is **Liouville integrable**
with one conserved quantity ($L$) matching the one degree of freedom of the map.

## 5. Chaotic Billiards: The Bunimovich Stadium

### Geometry

The **Bunimovich stadium** is the convex domain

$$\Omega_\text{st} = \{ (x,y) : -l/2 \leq x \leq l/2,\; |y| \leq r \}
\cup \{ (x,y) : (x \pm l/2)^2 + y^2 \leq r^2 \},$$

consisting of a rectangle of width $l$ and two semicircular caps of radius $r$.
The default in `DynamicalBilliards.jl` uses $l = 1$, $r = 0.5$.

### Defocusing mechanism

A circular arc (when viewed as the enclosing wall) is a **focusing** boundary: a
pencil of parallel rays converges after reflection.  Strikingly, Bunimovich proved
that even though the caps are circular, the **overall effect is defocusing**:

> A parallel beam hitting a semicircular cap diverges after the next straight-wall
> reflection.  The focusing at the cap and the defocusing at the flat wall combine
> with a net exponential divergence rate.

The key condition is that the arcs must be **complete semicircles** (subtending
exactly $\pi$ radians).  Shorter arcs fail to defocus completely, leaving islands of
stability.

### Bunimovich's theorem (1979)

**Theorem.** The stadium billiard is ergodic, mixing, a $K$-system, and Bernoulli.
In particular:

1. **Ergodicity** (time average = phase-space average):
   $$\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n(x)) = \int_\Sigma f\,\mathrm{d}\mu
   \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in \Sigma.$$

2. **Mixing** (loss of memory):
   $$\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu(T^n(A) \cap B) = \mu(A)\,\mu(B)
   \quad \forall\; A, B \subset \Sigma.$$

3. **Positive KS entropy** (see §7).

These properties hold for almost every initial condition (i.e., except for a set of
measure zero containing the unstable periodic orbits and their stable manifolds).

## 6. The Sinai Billiard

The **Sinai billiard** (1970) is a rectangular domain with a circular scatterer
removed:

$$\Omega_\text{S} = [0, a] \times [0, b] \;\setminus\; \overline{D(c, r)},$$

where $D(c,r)$ is an open disk of radius $r < \min(a,b)/2$ centred at $c$.

Unlike the stadium, where chaos arises from defocusing, the Sinai billiard uses a
**dispersing** scatterer.  A particle hitting the convex outside of the disk
experiences a divergent beam: nearby trajectories separate exponentially after each
collision with the disk.

### Sinai's theorem (1970)

**Theorem.** For any $r > 0$, the Sinai billiard is ergodic, mixing, and a
$K$-system.

This was the **first rigorous proof of ergodicity** for a specific Hamiltonian system
(previously ergodicity had been a statistical hypothesis, not a theorem).

### Curvature and hyperbolicity

The exponential divergence rate is related to the curvature $\kappa$ of the boundary.
For the circular scatterer of radius $r$, $\kappa = 1/r$.  After reflection off the
disk, a wavefront of curvature $K_\text{in}$ acquires curvature

$$K_\text{out} = K_\text{in} + \frac{2\kappa}{\cos\alpha},$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle of incidence.  Since $K_\text{out} > K_\text{in}$, each
disk collision increases the curvature (focuses the wavefront more strongly), and the
**Jacobi field** (infinitesimal separation between nearby orbits) grows without bound.

## 7. Lyapunov Exponents

### Definition

Let $\delta\mathbf{x}_0 = (\delta s_0, \delta p_0)$ be an infinitesimal perturbation
to an initial condition $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \Sigma$.  Its evolution under $DT^n$
(the tangent map at $\mathbf{x}_0$ after $n$ steps) defines the **finite-time
Lyapunov exponent**

$$\lambda^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}_0,\, \hat{\mathbf{e}}) =
\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\|DT^n(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}\|}{|\hat{\mathbf{e}}|},$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ is the initial direction of perturbation.  By Oseledec's
multiplicative ergodic theorem, the limit

$$\lambda_i = \lim_{n\to\infty} \lambda^{(n)} \quad (i = 1, 2)$$

exists for almost every $\mathbf{x}_0$ and (for non-degenerate initial directions)
takes two values $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2$.

### Symplectic constraint

Because $T$ is area-preserving, the Lyapunov exponents satisfy

$$\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 0, \qquad \lambda_1 \geq 0.$$

For a chaotic orbit $\lambda_1 > 0$ (the exponents are ±$\lambda_1$).  For an
integrable orbit all exponents vanish.

### Numerical spectrum

`DynamicalBilliards.jl` returns a **4-element** spectrum
$(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3, \lambda_4)$ because it augments the 2D map
with the continuous-time flow (adding two neutral directions from time translation):

$$\lambda_1 > 0, \quad \lambda_2 \approx 0, \quad \lambda_3 \approx 0,
\quad \lambda_4 = -\lambda_1 < 0.$$

The physical exponent is $\lambda_1$.  For the Bunimovich stadium with $l = r = 0.5$,
numerical computation gives $\lambda_1 \approx 0.85$.

### KS entropy and Pesin's formula

The **Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy** equals the sum of all positive Lyapunov exponents
(Pesin 1977):

$$h_\text{KS} = \sum_{\lambda_i > 0} \lambda_i = \lambda_1.$$

It measures the rate (in nats per collision) at which the system generates new
information / loses predictability.

### Predictability horizon

Two orbits with initial separation $\delta_0$ become indistinguishable from two
independent random trajectories (separation $\sim O(1)$) after approximately

$$n^* \approx \frac{\ln(\delta_\text{max}/\delta_0)}{\lambda_1}$$

collisions.  For the stadium with $\delta_0 = 10^{-5}$ and $\lambda_1 \approx 0.85$:

$$n^* \approx \frac{\ln 10^5}{0.85} \approx 14 \text{ collisions.}$$

This is demonstrated in `07_billiards_stadium.jl` with $\Delta\varphi_0 = 10^{-5}$.

## 8. Ergodicity, Mixing, and the Chaos Hierarchy

Dynamical systems admit a strict inclusion of increasingly strong statistical
properties:

$$\text{Integrable} \;\subsetneq\; \text{Ergodic} \;\subsetneq\;
\text{Mixing} \;\subsetneq\; K\text{-system} \;\subsetneq\; \text{Bernoulli}.$$

| Level | Defining property | Billiard example |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Integrable | All orbits on invariant tori; $\lambda_i = 0$ | Circle, ellipse, rectangle |
| Ergodic | $\frac{1}{N}\sum f(T^n x) \to \int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ a.e. | Stadium, Sinai |
| Mixing | $\mu(T^n A \cap B) \to \mu(A)\mu(B)$ | Stadium, Sinai |
| $K$-system | $h_\text{KS} > 0$; exponential decay of correlations | Stadium, Sinai |
| Bernoulli | Isomorphic (mod 0) to an i.i.d. process | Stadium, Sinai |

For an ergodic billiard, the Poincaré section fills the strip $\{(s,p):|p|<1\}$
**uniformly** (with respect to $\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}p$) for almost every initial
condition.  This is exactly what `08_billiards_poincare.jl` shows.

## 9. KAM Theory and Invariant Tori

### Twist condition

Near an integrable billiard (e.g., near-circular), the billiard map is a
**monotone twist map**: the rotation number $\varrho(p)$ is a strictly increasing
function of $p$,

$$\frac{\partial \varrho}{\partial p} > 0.$$

This twist condition is the hypothesis of the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem
and of the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem.

### KAM theorem (informal)

**Theorem.** Under a sufficiently small perturbation of an integrable billiard, most
invariant tori (those with sufficiently irrational rotation numbers satisfying a
Diophantine condition) survive, only slightly deformed.

The surviving tori form a **Cantor set** of positive measure.  The complement (a
set of small but positive measure) is filled with chaotic orbits.  As the perturbation
grows, more tori break, and the chaotic sea expands.

### Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem

At every rational rotation number $\varrho_0 = k/m$ of an unperturbed torus,
perturbation creates exactly $2m$ periodic orbits: $m$ elliptic (stable, surrounded
by small islands) and $m$ hyperbolic (unstable, connected by heteroclinic orbits).

### Why the stadium has no KAM tori

Bunimovich's proof rules out all invariant curves in the interior of phase space for
the stadium.  The only exception is the family of horizontal **bouncing-ball orbits**
(the particle bouncing vertically between the two flat walls), which is a set of
measure zero.

## 10. Simulation Notes

This section describes how `DynamicalBilliards.jl` computes the billiard trajectory
and what numerical errors arise.

### 10.1 Collision detection

The particle travels along the ray $\mathbf{q}(t) = \mathbf{q}_0 + t\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}$.
Collision with a boundary component is found by solving the intersection with each
obstacle algebraically (no numerical ODE integration).

**Circular obstacle** (disk of radius $R$, centre $\mathbf{c}$):

$$|\mathbf{q}_0 + t\hat{\mathbf{v}} - \mathbf{c}|^2 = R^2$$
$$\Longrightarrow \quad t^2 + 2t\,(\mathbf{q}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}) + |\mathbf{q}'|^2 - R^2 = 0,
\qquad \mathbf{q}' = \mathbf{q}_0 - \mathbf{c}.$$

Solving the quadratic (with $|\hat{\mathbf{v}}|=1$):

$$t = -(\mathbf{q}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}})
\pm \sqrt{(\mathbf{q}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}})^2 - |\mathbf{q}'|^2 + R^2}.$$

The particle is inside the billiard ($|\mathbf{q}'| < R$), so the discriminant is
strictly positive and the **larger root** gives the next boundary hit:

$$t_\text{hit} = -(\mathbf{q}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}})
+ \sqrt{(\mathbf{q}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}})^2 + R^2 - |\mathbf{q}'|^2}.$$

**Flat wall** (infinite line $y = y_w$, with $v_y \neq 0$):

$$t_\text{hit} = \frac{y_w - q_y}{v_y}.$$

For a finite wall segment, the hit is accepted only if the $x$-coordinate of the
impact point lies within the segment endpoints.

When multiple boundary components are present (stadium, Sinai), all candidate times
are computed and the **minimum positive** $t$ is taken:

$$t^* = \min\{ t_i > \varepsilon : \text{candidate } i \text{ is geometrically valid}\},$$

where $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-10}$ is a small threshold to avoid re-detecting the
current collision point.

### 10.2 Reflection and outward normal

**Flat wall** with outward normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = (0, \pm 1)$:

$$\mathbf{v}_\text{out} = (v_x,\, -v_y) \quad (\text{top/bottom wall})$$
$$\mathbf{v}_\text{out} = (-v_x,\, v_y) \quad (\text{left/right wall})$$

**Circular obstacle** at centre $\mathbf{c}$, hit point $\mathbf{q}_*$:

$$\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{\mathbf{q}_* - \mathbf{c}}{|\mathbf{q}_* - \mathbf{c}|},
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_\text{out} = \mathbf{v}_\text{in}
- 2(\mathbf{v}_\text{in}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}.$$

**Semicircle** (stadium cap): same formula with $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = (\mathbf{q}_* -
\mathbf{c}_\text{cap}) / r$.

### 10.3 Boundary conditions

The physical boundary condition is the **Dirichlet wall**: the particle may not
penetrate $\partial\Omega$.  For the billiard map this is implemented as:

1. Advance the particle to $\mathbf{q}_* = \mathbf{q}_0 + t^*\hat{\mathbf{v}}$.
2. Apply the reflection law to get $\mathbf{v}_\text{out}$.
3. Set the new state: $(\mathbf{q}_0, \hat{\mathbf{v}}) \leftarrow
   (\mathbf{q}_*, \mathbf{v}_\text{out})$.

There is **no periodic boundary** condition (the particle does not leave through one
wall and re-enter through the opposite one), unlike, e.g., a periodic Lorentz gas.
Each wall is a hard reflector.

### 10.4 Floating-point error and drift

Because trajectories between collisions are **exact straight lines**, there is no
integration error in the interior.  All round-off error accumulates at collision
points.  At each step, two sources contribute:

1. **Quadratic formula cancellation.** When $|\mathbf{q}'| \approx R$ (particle
   nearly on the circle), the square root $\sqrt{\Delta}$ is nearly zero and the
   formula $t = -b + \sqrt{\Delta}$ is subject to catastrophic cancellation.  Using
   the numerically stable form $t = -b + \sqrt{b^2 + R^2 - |\mathbf{q}'|^2}$ (where
   all terms are positive for an interior particle) avoids this.

2. **Normal direction drift.** After $n$ collisions the velocity magnitude may drift
   from 1.  The accumulated error is $||\hat{\mathbf{v}}_n| - 1| = O(n\varepsilon_M)$
   where $\varepsilon_M \approx 2.2\times10^{-16}$ is machine epsilon.  In practice
   `DynamicalBilliards.jl` does not re-normalise at each step; for $n \leq 10^6$
   the drift is $\lesssim 10^{-10}$ and negligible.

3. **Shadow trajectory and Lyapunov growth.** For a chaotic billiard the physical
   trajectory is shadowed by a true trajectory only for $n \lesssim n^*$
   (the predictability horizon, §7).  Beyond $n^*$, the computed orbit is not a
   good approximation to any individual true orbit — but it is still a typical
   orbit for statistical purposes (ergodic theory).

### 10.5 Birkhoff coordinates: `boundarymap`

`DynamicalBilliards.jl`'s `boundarymap(particle, billiard, n)` computes $(s_k, p_k)$
for each collision $k = 1, \ldots, n$.

**Arc-length $s_k$** is accumulated by summing the lengths of each obstacle traversed,
starting from a reference point.  For a piecewise boundary with obstacles
$\{\Gamma_j\}$:

$$s = \underbrace{\sum_{j < j^*} |\Gamma_j|}_{\text{preceding obstacles}}
+ \underbrace{s_\text{local}(\mathbf{q}_*)}_{\text{position within obstacle}},$$

where $s_\text{local}$ is measured from the start of $\Gamma_{j^*}$.

**Momentum $p_k$** is computed as the dot product of the post-reflection velocity
with the CCW unit tangent at the collision point:

$$p_k = \mathbf{v}_\text{out} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{q}_*),
\qquad \hat{\mathbf{t}} = R_{+\pi/2}\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}.$$

Since the tangential component is preserved by reflection,
$\mathbf{v}_\text{out}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}} = \mathbf{v}_\text{in}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}$,
so either velocity (before or after) gives the same $p$.

The `intervals` return value from `boundarymap` stores the cumulative arc-lengths
$[0, |\Gamma_1|, |\Gamma_1|+|\Gamma_2|, \ldots, L]$, which is used to normalise the
$s$-axis in the Poincaré section plot.

### 10.6 Lyapunov spectrum: `lyapunovspectrum`

`lyapunovspectrum(particle, billiard, n)` integrates the **tangent map** alongside
the trajectory.  At each collision the $2\times2$ Jacobian $DT$ is evaluated
analytically and multiplied into a running product:

$$M_n = DT(\mathbf{x}_{n-1})\cdots DT(\mathbf{x}_0).$$

The Lyapunov exponents are extracted from $M_n$ via a QR decomposition (Benettin
algorithm) to avoid exponential overflow:

$$\lambda_i = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\ln r_{ii}^{(n)},$$

where $r_{ii}^{(n)}$ are the diagonal entries of the upper-triangular factor of the
$n$-step QR decomposition.

Convergence is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ for ergodic systems.  For the values quoted in
`07_billiards_stadium.jl`, $n = 5000$ collisions (averaged over 10 initial conditions)
gives $\lambda_1$ accurate to roughly two significant figures.
